\chapter{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{chap:experiment}

\section{Evaluation Objectives}
\label{sec:eval-objectives}

The methods described in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} are evaluated to answer four
questions, each primarily varying one factor relative to a shared anchor configuration
(LeNet, CIFAR-10, batch size 1, no defense); where a method's own requirements force an
additional change, this is noted in that RQ's own discussion below:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{RQ1.} Under a matched architecture, dataset, and batch size, how do an
  optimization-based attack (DLG/iDLG) and a closed-form attack (R-GAP) compare in
  reconstruction quality and runtime?
  \item \textbf{RQ2.} Does an optimization-based attack designed for scale (Inverting
  Gradients) still succeed when the victim architecture is deeper and closer to what is
  deployed in practice?
  \item \textbf{RQ3.} How does each attack's reconstruction quality change when the
  leaked gradient is averaged over a batch of two private images instead of one?
  \item \textbf{RQ4.} Can a standard gradient-perturbation defense (DP-SGD) suppress
  each attack, and does the noise level required to do so differ between attack
  families?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Evaluation Scenarios}
\label{sec:eval-scenarios}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval-metrics}

Reconstruction quality is reported with two standard image-similarity metrics,
computed in pixel space ($[0,1]$ range) between the reconstructed image $\hat{x}$ and
the ground-truth image $x$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{d}\lVert \hat{x} - x \rVert_2^2,
\qquad
\mathrm{PSNR} = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{MSE}}\right) \; \text{(dB)}.
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
Higher PSNR (equivalently, lower MSE) indicates a reconstruction closer to the
original image; a PSNR near or below $0$~dB indicates the reconstruction is no more
informative than noise. The ground truth $x$ is always in $[0,1]$, but the
reconstruction $\hat{x}$ is not clamped to that range before these metrics are
computed: under a failed or diverged attack (most visibly in the DP-SGD sweeps of
RQ4), $\hat{x}$ can fall far outside $[0,1]$, which is what produces PSNR values far
below $0$~dB rather than a bounded worst case. LeNet images are already in $[0,1]$, so
Equation~\eqref{eq:metrics} applies directly. The ResNet-18 notebooks normalize inputs
with ImageNet statistics and denormalize displayed images with CIFAR-10 statistics.
The ResNet MSE and PSNR in Sections~\ref{sec:rq2} and~\ref{sec:rq3} are peak-1
metrics after that denormalization. A GPU rerun measured the normalized-tensor MSE
separately, at $0.301$ for batch size 1 and $0.375$ for batch size 2; the
corresponding denormalized MSE values were $0.0184$ and $0.0233$. The notebook PSNR
helper also inserts $1/\mathrm{std}$ inside the logarithm and returned $17.23$~dB
and $16.29$~dB, next to the peak-1 values $17.35$~dB and $16.33$~dB.
Two secondary metrics are used where
applicable: \textbf{label
accuracy} (whether the attacker's recovered label matches the ground-truth label,
relevant to DLG/iDLG and Inverting Gradients, which recover a label as part of the
attack) and \textbf{gradient cosine similarity} between the defended and original
gradient (used in the DP-SGD sweeps below as a direct measure of how
much signal the defense removes, independent of whether the attack converges).

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:eval-results}

\subsection{RQ1 -- Baseline Comparison on a Shared Architecture}
\label{sec:rq1}

DLG (Equation~\eqref{eq:dlg-objective}), iDLG, Inverting Gradients
(Equation~\eqref{eq:ig-objective}), and R-GAP (Equation~\eqref{eq:rgap-weight-grad})
are each run once on the shared LeNet configuration of Table~\ref{tab:eval-setup}
(the LeNetRGAP variant for R-GAP), batch size 1, no defense: the same
dataset, the same image, and the same convolutional topology for all four
attacks. R-GAP's closed-form derivation forces two further changes beyond
this shared setup: the bias/activation difference of LeNetRGAP, and a
single-logit, binary-logistic output layer in place of the 10-way
cross-entropy classification task that DLG/iDLG and Inverting Gradients
attack (Section~\ref{sec:architectures}); R-GAP is therefore compared
under a matched architecture and image, but not a fully identical task.
The comparison reports reconstruction quality (MSE, PSNR) for each method.

\bottomrule

R-GAP's closed-form solve reproduces the image to within floating-point
rounding error, confirmed visually in Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}: its
reconstruction is indistinguishable from the ground truth. DLG and iDLG
converge to a close but visibly noisy approximation after 300 iterations
of optimization, and Inverting Gradients -- tuned for scale rather than
precision on small networks -- trails both on this LeNet configuration.
A later GPU rerun (Python 3.12.12, PyTorch 2.14, RTX 4060) rebuilt this LeNet
from seed 50. The DLG notebook and the Inverting Gradients LeNet notebook
produced identical weights, and Inverting Gradients reproduced the stored
result at MSE $0.0023$ and PSNR $26.38$~dB. DLG and iDLG again recovered
label 3, but L-BFGS did not retrace the stored PyTorch 2.10 trajectory:
MSE was $0.00408$ and $0.00385$. Table~\ref{tab:rq1} therefore reports the
stored notebook, not a bit-identical rerun of that L-BFGS path.
The stored R-GAP baseline reports 57.1~s, compared with 532.355~s for
Inverting Gradients on LeNet. R-GAP has no per-pixel optimization loop
(Section~\ref{sec:rgap}), but still performs an iterative scalar solve at
the output layer and constructs and solves convolutional linear systems
on the CPU. Without separately instrumented stages and a common timing
protocol for all attacks, these observations do not establish a general
runtime ranking or which stage dominates R-GAP's runtime.

\paragraph{Separate CPU verification.} A subsequent Python 3.12.12 CPU run
measured 75.91~s for this baseline. A separately instrumented repeat took
84.12~s, including 2.99~s for scalar Adam and 78.49~s for the four
least-squares solves. These are not replacements for historical timings,
but contradict attributing nearly all runtime to the scalar solve.
Before casting the reconstructed image to float32, this repeat obtained
MSE $2.72\times10^{-22}$ and PSNR 215.66~dB; the reported infinity arises
after the float32 cast makes the measured error zero.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-milestones} tracks how DLG and iDLG reach that final
reconstruction: both start from the same random noise and converge toward
the ground truth over the 300-iteration L-BFGS run, with iDLG's
single-variable optimization visibly settling into the right shape a few
milestones earlier than DLG's joint image-and-label search.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rq1_milestones.png}
\caption{DLG (top) and iDLG (bottom) reconstructions at 10 evenly-spaced
iterations of the same optimization run shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}.}
\label{fig:rq1-milestones}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Numerical precision.} In the historical run, repeating the attack in
float32 (PyTorch's default), with identical weights but gradients recomputed
in float32, yields
MSE $=91.25$, PSNR $=-19.60$~dB -- pure noise. Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-float}
localizes the failure: the float32 and float64 reconstructions agree
closely through all four intermediate layers, and diverge only at the
final step (recovering the image itself from the first convolutional
layer's input), where the smallest singular value of that layer's linear
system is small enough for float32 rounding error to be amplified into
dominant noise. DLG and iDLG have no equivalent failure mode at this
precision, since gradient descent is not solving an exact linear system at
any step.

The separate CPU verification instead reached 66.57~dB in the float32
test and did not reproduce this collapse. The historical figure therefore
illustrates a run-specific numerical failure, not proof that float32
necessarily destroys the attack on this architecture.

\subsection{RQ2 -- Scaling to a Deeper Architecture}
\label{sec:rq2}

Inverting Gradients (Equation~\eqref{eq:ig-objective}) is run on an untrained
ResNet-18, 11.7M-parameter victim model, on an ImageNet sample image at
$224\times224$ resolution: a change of architecture, dataset, and image
resolution together from RQ1's LeNet/CIFAR-10 setting, since a meaningful
test of ResNet-18 requires input at the scale it is actually deployed at,
an order of magnitude larger than RQ1's LeNet configuration.
Section~\ref{sec:inverting-gradients} already explains, at the level of the
optimization objective, why the cosine cost and sign-based gradient update
were introduced specifically to make this scale tractable; this section
reports whether that design succeeds in practice, by the same MSE/PSNR
metrics as RQ1.

With 4 random restarts of 24{,}000 iterations each, the stored run reaches
a denormalized MSE of $0.0185$ and a peak-1 PSNR of $17.33$~dB
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-metrics}). The GPU rerun of the same configuration
gave denormalized MSE $0.01840$ and peak-1 PSNR $17.35$~dB. Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}
shows the reconstruction is visibly identifiable (pose, fur color, collar
pattern) despite the much larger parameter count and image resolution than
RQ1's LeNet setting, confirming that the cosine cost and sign-based gradient update
are sufficient to keep the attack tractable at this scale -- though at
markedly lower fidelity than RQ1's Inverting Gradients result on the
smaller LeNet (PSNR 26.48~dB; Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).
The reconstruction box uses CIFAR-10 statistics while the input was normalized
with ImageNet statistics. Projecting the beagle onto that box changes the true
pixels by MSE $1.4\times10^{-4}$, about $39$~dB, so the mismatched box is not
what limits the $17$~dB reconstruction. Denormalizing the ground truth itself
with the CIFAR-10 constants changes the displayed image by MSE $0.00163$.

\subsection{RQ3 -- Robustness to Batch Size}
\label{sec:rq3}

Each attack is compared against its own batch-size-1 result when the leaked gradient
instead averages two private images: DLG/iDLG, Inverting Gradients (on its ResNet-18
configuration only; the shared-LeNet configuration is not swept at batch size 2), and
R-GAP. R-GAP is evaluated on both victim architectures
from Table~\ref{tab:eval-setup}, since Section~\ref{sec:rgap} already
identifies a specific, architecture-dependent
reason batch size might affect it differently from the optimization-based attacks:
its recursive equations assume a single sample, and at batch size $>1$ a convolutional
architecture additionally imposes forward-pass constraints that a purely
fully-connected one (FCN3) does not. This RQ tests whether that predicted asymmetry
(LeNetRGAP vs.\ FCN3) is actually observed, alongside the batch-size degradation of
the two optimization-based attacks.

Both optimization-based attacks lose roughly 11--12~dB going from batch 1
to batch 2 on LeNet, while Inverting Gradients on ResNet-18 loses under
1~dB -- its batch-1 result is already far from perfect at this scale
(RQ2), leaving less margin left to lose. The GPU rerun recovered the same
DLG/iDLG label pair $[2,7]$ and a denormalized ResNet batch-2 PSNR of
$16.33$~dB, against the stored $16.35$~dB. Its DLG and iDLG mean MSE values
were $0.0273$ and $0.0253$, above the stored $0.0235$ and $0.0216$, the same
L-BFGS discrepancy as in Section~\ref{sec:rq1}. Figure~\ref{fig:rq3-ig-batch2}
shows both ResNet-18 batch-2 reconstructions remain individually
identifiable.

\paragraph{R-GAP at batch size 2: the linear-combination property.}
With $B=2$, the leaked weight gradient is an average over both images, and
Equation~\eqref{eq:rgap-weight-grad} no longer uniquely determines either
input on its own. On a network with no convolutional layers (FCN3), each
layer's recovered input comes only from inverting the weight-gradient
equation~\eqref{eq:rgap-weight-grad} (linear in that layer's input) and,
exactly, that layer's own invertible activation, so the recursive solution
R-GAP recovers is the \emph{average} of the two inputs,
$\tfrac{1}{2}(x_A + x_B)$ -- regardless of FCN3's LeakyReLU nonlinearity,
since R-GAP inverts it exactly at every layer rather than assuming it away.
A convolutional architecture adds the extra forward-pass constraints of
Equation~\eqref{eq:rgap-forward} at every layer, which the blended,
two-image gradient signal does not consistently satisfy -- so the property
fails not because of nonlinearity, but because a convolution imposes a
constraint beyond the weight-gradient equation alone. Table~\ref{tab:rq3-rgap}
confirms this: on FCN3, R-GAP's final-stage output is highly correlated
with the plain pixel average in \emph{direction}; this batch-2 solve does
not separately fix the overall sign and scale of its solution (unlike the
batch-1 case, where the recovered image matched the ground truth exactly),
so cosine similarity establishes agreement in direction rather than in
absolute pixel value. On LeNetRGAP (CNN), it is uncorrelated with either
individual image or their average.

At batch size 1, R-GAP reconstructs this same architecture's input exactly
(Section~\ref{sec:rq1}); at batch size 2 the CNN variant's reconstruction
is uncorrelated with either image or their average ($|\cos|\approx0.06$ in
Table~\ref{tab:rq3-rgap}). A GPU rerun reproduced those cosines to four
decimals: $0.0608$ for LeNetRGAP against the average, and $1.0000$ for FCN3.
This is a sharper collapse than either optimization-based
attack shows going from batch 1 to batch 2.

\subsection{RQ4 -- Effectiveness of the DP-SGD Defense}
\label{sec:rq4}

The DP-SGD-style defense of Equation~\eqref{eq:dpsgd} -- clip-and-noise applied once to
the already-aggregated gradient, not the per-example, training-integrated mechanism of
\cite{abadi2016dpsgd} itself (Section~\ref{sec:dpsgd}) -- is applied to the leaked
gradient before each attack ever observes it, sweeping clipping thresholds $C = 0.5\lVert
g\rVert$ and $C = 1.0\lVert g\rVert$ (half the gradient's own norm, and the exact
boundary at which clipping has no effect) together with a grid of noise multipliers
$\sigma$. Thresholds are set relative to each configuration's own gradient norm so
that clipping strength is comparable across attacks whose gradients live at very
different absolute scales. Inverting Gradients is swept on both the shared LeNet
(for a direct, matched-architecture comparison against DLG/iDLG and R-GAP) and
ResNet-18 (to check whether the defense still holds at scale); R-GAP uses a wider
range of noise multipliers (Section~\ref{sec:dpsgd}), since its sensitivity to
numerical perturbation would otherwise place every sampled $\sigma$ past its
breaking point. Each configuration here also runs fewer optimization iterations
than the undefended baselines of RQ1/RQ2, so the $\sigma=0$ row below is a
lower-budget reference point, not directly comparable to those undefended numbers.

For example, Inverting Gradients on LeNet reaches 27.94~dB in the
zero-noise, no-clip sweep, versus 26.48~dB in RQ1. A paired GPU ablation
kept the same weights, original gradient, and true label, and reseeded
before every trial. With median filtering every 500 steps, 4{,}800
iterations scored 27.85~dB and 24{,}000 iterations scored 26.36~dB.
Without the filter those budgets scored 16.33~dB and 22.06~dB. The higher
defense PSNR is the shorter median-filtered budget, not an improvement from
clipping or noise. Median filtering raises PSNR relative to the unfiltered
run; extending the filtered run from 4{,}800 to 24{,}000 steps lowers it.

At the LeNet configuration shared by DLG/iDLG and Inverting Gradients, clipping
alone already separates the two cost functions as Section~\ref{sec:inverting-gradients}
predicts: iDLG's $L_2$ cost is sensitive to the gradient's absolute magnitude, so
$C=0.5\lVert g\rVert$ collapses it even at $\sigma=0$ (PSNR $-105$~dB), while
Inverting Gradients' scale-invariant cosine cost gives nearly identical PSNR at
both clipping settings (Table~\ref{tab:rq4-lenet}). Increasing $\sigma$ then
degrades both attacks, but at different rates: iDLG collapses sharply between
$\sigma=10^{-4}$ and $\sigma=10^{-3}$, while Inverting Gradients on the same
architecture stays above 17~dB at $\sigma=10^{-3}$ -- roughly an order of
magnitude more noise tolerance. On ResNet-18, Inverting Gradients' defended PSNR
stays in a narrow 9--13~dB band across the full grid, close to its $\sigma=0$
value of 12.72~dB -- the defense adds little further degradation beyond what the
reduced iteration count already costs.

\paragraph{R-GAP under DP-SGD.} Table~\ref{tab:rq4-rgap} reports R-GAP's own,
finer-grained sweep (LeNetRGAP, same image as RQ1). In the no-clip row, the attack is exact at
$\sigma=0$ and still reaches 31.0~dB at $\sigma=10^{-7}$, but collapses to
$-19.5$~dB at $\sigma=10^{-6}$ -- just one order of magnitude more noise.
This breaking point sits three to four orders of magnitude below iDLG's
($\sigma\approx10^{-3}$--$10^{-4}$) on a comparable LeNet architecture: the
noise level needed to defeat R-GAP leaves DLG/iDLG's reconstruction
visibly unaffected. This matches Section~\ref{sec:rgap}'s mechanism -- an
exact recursive solve cannot average out perturbation the way gradient
descent does across hundreds of steps -- giving DP-SGD a use it otherwise
lacks: a noise level too small to touch the optimization-based attacks
already defeats R-GAP outright.

These breaking points describe the historical noise realizations, not
universal thresholds. In the separate CPU sweep, nominal no clipping
retained 14.66~dB at $\sigma=10^{-6}$, whereas half-norm clipping produced
$-14.61$~dB there. Repeated, paired-noise trials are needed before
attributing either ordering to clipping-induced numerical conditioning.
